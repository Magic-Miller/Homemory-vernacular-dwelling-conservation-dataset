# Field Photography Guide

Anyone with a smartphone can contribute. Follow the steps below to produce photographs that can be used without unnecessary reshooting.

## 1. Before You Start

- Ask for permission before entering or photographing private property, occupied interiors, or people.
- Do not enter unstable or restricted areas for a better photograph.
- Do not touch, clean, remove, probe, or otherwise disturb building materials.
- Clean the phone camera lens before shooting.

## 2. Phone Camera Settings

- Use the phone's original camera app.
- Use the phone's standard **12 MP** photo mode when available.
- The minimum accepted image size is **2000 × 1500 pixels** in landscape orientation, or **1500 × 2000 pixels** in portrait orientation, which is approximately **3 megapixels**.
- A larger original image, such as **4000 × 3000 pixels (12 MP)**, is recommended when available.
- The standard **4:3 aspect ratio** is recommended because it often uses more of the phone camera sensor, but it is not required.
- Other common aspect ratios, including **16:9, 3:2, and 1:1**, are accepted if the image meets the minimum pixel dimensions, remains clear, and does not crop out important building or defect context.
- Both landscape and portrait orientation are accepted. Choose the orientation that best shows the building component and the complete visible defect.
- Save and upload the original full-resolution image in **JPG, JPEG, or PNG** format.
- Do not use filters, screenshots, portrait effects, or compressed copies.
- Do not resize, resample, or artificially enlarge the image.
- Avoid digital zoom. Move closer only when it is safe to do so.

If you are unsure about the settings, use the phone's normal `Photo` mode and upload the original image at full size. Most standard smartphone settings already exceed the minimum requirement. You do not need to adjust the aspect ratio or DPI settings on the phone.

## 3. Photograph the Building First

Before taking close-ups, photograph the building and its main spaces so that later reviewers can understand where each defect is located.

For a typical case, aim for **5–10 overview and context photographs**, including as many of the following as are accessible:

- the whole building and its surroundings;
- the main exterior elevations;
- the entrance, courtyard, hall, or other main spaces;
- an overall view of the roof, walls, doors, windows, or structural system.

Keep the camera level, avoid excessive tilting, and include enough surroundings. Take at least one overall photograph of each important space.

## 4. Photograph Each Visible Defect

For each defect, usually take **two photographs**:

1. **Context photograph:** stand about **1–2 metres away** when possible. Place the defect near the centre and show its relationship to the surrounding wall, roof, window, wall base, drainage, timber, or other component.
2. **Close-up photograph:** move closer and clearly show the defect's texture, edges, crack pattern, staining, material loss, biological growth, or later repair.

When size matters, add a third photograph with a ruler or another safe scale reference. Do not cover the defect or damage the surface when placing the reference.

Take additional views when a crack is long, a surface is bulging or leaning, material may fall, timber damage is extensive, or the defect is closely related to roofing, drainage, pipes, or the wall base.

A typical complete case may contain around **5–15 defect photographs**, depending on the number and complexity of visible problems. Useful coverage is more important than taking many nearly identical images.

## 5. Check Every Photograph

Before leaving the site, zoom in on the photograph and confirm that:

- it is sharp and not blurred;
- the defect is well lit and clearly visible;
- the close-up shows detail;
- the context photograph shows where the defect is located;
- fingers, objects, or scale references do not block the defect;
- no important area has been accidentally cropped out.

Retake the photograph immediately if any of these checks fail.

## 6. Record Basic Information

For each group of photographs, record:

- the general location and photography date;
- whether the photograph is interior or exterior;
- the building component, if known;
- one sentence describing only what is visible;
- whether people or private information appear;
- whether research use and public release are permitted.

Building history, current use, local names, family memories, and community stories are encouraged but optional.

## 7. Privacy and Professional Boundaries

- Avoid recognizable faces, children's faces, house numbers, exact addresses, contact information, private documents, family photographs, and private daily-life details unless specific permission has been obtained.
- Flag photographs that may require redaction instead of publishing them directly.
- Describe only what you can see. Do not guess the cause, assess structural safety, or recommend repairs.

## Final Checklist

Before submitting, confirm:

- permission and safety requirements were followed;
- the building has overview photographs;
- each important defect has a context photograph and a close-up;
- images are at least **2000 × 1500 pixels** or larger and are in focus;
- original full-resolution files are being submitted;
- privacy and publication permissions have been recorded.
